Store Price-Matching Algorithm

Python · ML · Data Analysis

View on GitHub →

An algorithm that matches products across two grocery store catalogs, even when the same item is named, branded, and sized differently in each.

~343,000,000 possible product pairs
→
1 best match per product

The Problem

To price-match, a business has to work out which of its products are the same as a competitor's. That is harder than it sounds: product names, brands, and sizes rarely line up between stores. One catalog might list a "6 x 8 oz" pack while another lists the same item as "48 oz", and brand and wording vary too.

The two catalogs here held about 36,900 and 9,300 products. Comparing every product against every other means roughly 343 million pairs, far too many to score carefully one by one.

tldr: Brute force time complexity sucks (O(n^2))! We want O(n log n) or better!

The Intuition

Suppose one store sells Coca-Cola Classic Soda, 12 oz cans, 12 pack, and the other store has these three products. As a shopper, which would you say is the closest match?

Other store's productWordingSizeBrand
A. Coca-Cola Classic Soda, 12 oz cans, 24 packNearly identicalTwice as muchSame
B. Coca-Cola Classic, 12 fl oz cans (12 ct)Quite differentSameSame
C. Pepsi Cola Soda, 12 oz cans, 12 packSimilarSameDifferent

Most shoppers would pick B. It's worded differently, but it is the same drink in the same amount. Product A reads almost exactly like the original but holds twice as much soda, so it sells at a different price. Product C is the right size but a different brand.

No single signal gets this right. Comparing wording alone picks A. Brand alone can't tell A from B, and size alone can't tell B from C. Only by looking at all three together does B come out on top, and some signals matter more than others, which is why I chose to implement a heuristic function.

Scoring

Each candidate pair gets a final score that combines three signals:

  • Text similarity (60%). The TF-IDF cosine similarity between the two products' text.
  • Packaging (30%). How close the total sizes are, scored from 0 to 1. If the units are incompatible the score is 0, and if either side is missing a size it falls back to comparing pack counts.
  • Brand (10%). 1 if the brands match exactly, otherwise 0.

Blocking is done in batches with sparse matrix operations, which keeps memory use manageable across the full catalogs.

Results

~93× fewer pairs to score
< 1 min end-to-end runtime
36,559 Store A products matched
  • Blocking did the heavy lifting. Keeping the top 100 candidates per product cut about 343 million possible pairs to about 3.7 million—about 1% of the total—so the expensive scoring only ran on a small fraction of them.
  • Fast enough. The full pipeline runs in under a minute. A brute-force version that scored every pair never finishes.
  • Every product got a match. Each Store A product received exactly one best Store B match, 36,559 in total.
  • Accurate results. Looking through the output manually, the matches fit human intuition near perfectly.

Course Design

Pedagogy · assessment · lesson plans

The thinking behind the worksheets: how I structure assessment, note-taking, and individual lessons. This is the best place to start if you want to know why the materials are shaped the way they are.

The Question Compass

A study tool built around one question: 'Do I really understand this?'. Here we take one idea and ask about it from six directions. If you can answer all six, you're likely heading in the right direction. If you can't, you've found exactly what to study, before a test finds it for you. This tool is to help students develop stronger metacognitive skills.

DirectionThe moveExample (idea: coefficient)
WhatWhat is it, in your own words?What is a coefficient?
Example / non-exampleGive one of each. Why is the non-example not one?Why isn't the 2 in x² a coefficient?
ReverseStart from an answer and work backwards.Write an expression where the coefficient of y is −1.
Break itFind the edge case. When does the idea get weird?Is there a term with no coefficient? Are you sure?
ConnectHow does it relate to another idea you know?How do coefficients relate to terms?
DisguiseHow would a test hide this idea inside a harder problem?Where could a coefficient question hide inside an equation problem?

The 3-Week Test Cycle

An assessment system meant to train students to generate questions, test themselves, and fool-proof their own work.

WeekActivitySkill being trained
1Question Compass drillsAsking about one idea from different directions
2Error autopsy and predict-the-testFool-proofing, and thinking like the examiner
3Test swap: each student writes the other's testEverything, with ownership
  • The test swap. Each student writes a test for the other. Students are graded on how they did on the test they took and on the quality of the test they wrote.
  • Error autopsy. Students get a fake student's worksheet with a few planted mistakes, including one subtle one, and have to find, explain, and fix each.
  • The big test, redesigned. Students get a blueprint of question types, and about half the questions are adapted from their own predictions and swap-test questions.

Note-taking

Taking notes is a skill in its own right. Good notes aren't a transcript: they record the exact definition (verbatim, because precision matters in math), the intuition in the student's own words, and a worked example to return to when stuck.

The plan releases responsibility gradually over three lessons. In the first, students fill in a guided sheet with labeled boxes. In the second, they get only the section headers. From the third lesson on they use blank paper, and each class opens with a five-minute comparison of notebooks. Download the note-taking plan (.docx)
